LarkOS

LarkOS is free community firmware for the Polyend Tracker Mini. It puts your project's key under every note, adds a live granular send that listens to the input and a tape machine to the master bus. It brings eight exciting new instruments including a weird and wonderful dual wavetable synth, a classic rompler player, a faithfully-recreated tonewheel organ, and fully synthesized 808 and 909 kits.

It is currently in closed beta testing and is scheduled to be released mid-December. A YouTube video introduction to follow in the coming week or two.

Stock, plus

LarkOS is a fork of Polyend Tracker Community Edition and built specifically for the Tracker Mini. Nothing stock was taken out, and every new setting starts where the Tracker already was. If you never press a harmony button and never switch on an effect, it behaves like CE, apart from MIDI notes landing in the project's key, which is the thing that started all of this.

Stock CE opens LarkOS projects: LarkOS adds no fields to the project format, and Beads and Cassette settings live in a small file beside the project, which CE simply ignores. Chords are the exception. Stock firmware's chord table is shorter, so chords that use LarkOS's richer entries won't play as written there. Going back to stock is the same firmware update as coming here, with nothing to undo first.

Project key is under everything

Set the scale and root note in Config › Project Settings and every note changes diatonically. Every harmony function follows, which live in the pattern editor on the pages behind More, and none of them opens a menu.

Chords

Press a chord. Press it again for its next color.

The chords, colors and voicings come from LarkOS's harmony code, run on a computer for these two keys. The pink key is the bass note. The sound is a plain browser tone, not a Tracker instrument.

A chord is one step on one track

A chord lives as FX data on a single step, not spread across tracks. The note column holds the chord's lowest note, and the chord FX beside it holds a number that picks an entry from the firmware's chord table. Each entry is the list of notes to sound above that lowest note, so inversions and voicings are simply different entries, such as "root 5 above the bass".

When the pattern plays:

  • On a synth or MIDI instrument, that one track plays the whole chord.
  • On a sample instrument, which has only one voice per track, LarkOS borrows the voices of idle tracks while it plays. Those tracks' steps stay empty in the pattern, and nothing is written to them.

The only exception is playing chords in from a MIDI keyboard with several tracks selected, which puts one note on each track, as stock always has. With just one step selected, it writes the chord-FX step instead.

Note: Stock firmware has a chord FX too, but its table stops at entry 67: triads, sevenths and their inversions. The 9ths, 11ths, 13ths, add9 and 6/9 chords and the shell and open voicings are LarkOS's entries 68 to 134, so a project that uses them won't play as written on stock firmware.

Notes that stay in the key

Where this all started: with a scale chosen, +1 and −1 move a note along the scale. In C major, C goes to D, E and F, never C#. A chord moves to the chord on the next degree, and +10 and −10 are still an octave. Notes from a MIDI keyboard (including the KB-1!) snap to the key. A Chromatic project, a track set to Drums, and empty steps work exactly as stock.

Seven chords on seven buttons

The Chords page holds the key's seven chords, each showing its numeral and its name. Press one and it is written at the cursor, press it again for its next color: Dm7, Dm9, Dm11, Dm13, Dm. A color is fitted to the degree it lands on, so on iii, where a ninth would leave the key, Em7 goes straight on to its eleventh.

Colors and voicings on the value keys

Put the cursor on a chord's FX value in edit mode. +1 and −1 walk its colors, up through add9, 6, 6/9, 7th, 9th, 11th and 13th and down to the triad and the suspensions. +10 and −10 step through close voicings, the inversions, shell and open voicings. You hear each one as a preview and the title bar names it: Chord: Dm9/F 1st inv.

Double-press Shift and LarkOS chooses

It looks 32 steps either way along the track: at the chords before and after (what they share, how rich they are, whether this one resolves into the next, how far the hand moves) and at the single notes nearby, which the chord should contain and not rub against. Double-press again for the runner-up, and again for a third. With a selection, it does every chord inside.

Play chords in from a keyboard

With one step selected, hold three or more keys on a MIDI keyboard. They are written as that one step, showing the lowest note while the chord FX registers what you played, inversion included. Stock CE sounded the extra notes but wrote only the first. Two keys a fourth or a fifth apart count as a chord too!

Chord tools and per-pattern Key

"Set" swaps the seven buttons between the key's own chords, chords borrowed from the parallel key. "Function" chooses between: five secondary dominants, the tritone substitute and the sharp-four diminished. "Voicing" chooses close, shell or open. "Bass" chooses the least movement, the root, third or fifth, or a pedal on the key note. "Transp" moves chords by step. "Pin Key" gives one pattern a key of its own, making things like modal interchange and pivots to parallel major/minor a breeze!

Chords on samples

A sample track has one voice, but the sample instrument has eight. A chord on a sample instrument borrows voices from tracks with nothing to play, empty tracks first. With three tracks free a seventh chord is complete, with fewer it keeps the third and seventh and drops the fifth first. A lent voice goes back the moment its own track plays.

Live granular processing and a vintage tape machine

Two new master bus effects are off until you use them, a project that never touches them sounds exactly as it did. The examples below are the firmware's own Cassette code and its implementation of Émilie Gillet's amazing Beads granular module.

Beads

Beads chops what you feed it into grains and scatters them...including whatever you send through the audio in jack, turning your Tracker Mini into a live granular pedal! Beads sits beside the delay and the reverb as a fourth send, so the drums can have a tail while the bass stays dry. Raise a track's Beads send, or write step FX Z to set it from that step on.

Time, Size, Shape and Pitch each have a random spread; then come Density, Feedback, Reverb, a choice of four Media, and the Send volume. Pitch moves in fourths, fifths and octaves, and its random spread sends each grain to one of them, never in between. Freeze stops writing to the buffer: mute everything underneath and the cloud carries on alone. Beads also renders as its own stem into your DAW.

Cassette

A vintage tape machine between the mix and the limiter, so everything goes through it: all eight tracks, the sends and the input. It is an insert, not a send, because wow and flutter bend pitch and a bent copy beside an unbent one is a flanger, not a tape deck.

Drive, Wow, Flutter, Low cut, High cut, Hiss, Dropout and Wet. Drive is matched in level, so turning it up thickens the sound rather than making it louder, and switching the tape in does not jump the volume. Off is bit-identical to having no Cassette at all. Step FX W sets the wet mix and holds until something changes it, so a breakdown can fall onto tape and the chorus come back off it.

Eight new instruments and an actually usable randomizer

Each is a plugin in /System/Synths and shows up in an instrument's Model list and each ships with dozens of presets. An intelligent randomizer tweaks parameters in seasonable ranges to produce usable sounds, not noisy garble ;)

x0x: an 808 and a 909, synthesised

Eleven drums: kick, snare, clap, rim, closed and open hat, ride, cymbal and three toms. Each can be the 808's or the 909's, drum by drum, so a 909 kick can sit under an 808 snare.

Each drum is built the way its circuit builds it and a fitting tool played each model against a recording of the real drum on three spectrograms and kept every change that brought them closer.

Twelve kits: 808, 909, 808 Long, 909 Tight, House, Electro, Deep, Toy, Soft, Lo-Fi, Crushed and Driven.

Per drum: Machine, Level, Pan, Tune, Attack, Decay, Tone and Snap. Across the kit: Grit, an overdrive run at twice the sample rate so what it adds stays in tune. Sample Rate and Bits reduction controls included to get crispy with it. Keys follow the General MIDI drum map, or a row of eleven from any key you choose.

  • Soli

    A famous string machine: twelve top-octave oscillators divided down, a three-phase ensemble across the lot, and the solo voices such machines carried. Paraphonic, like the real one, so octaves lock.

    Pad Full Ensemble Lead Violin Bass Synth Bass Keys String Piano
    51 presets

  • June

    A high-fidelity build of a certain six-voice polysynth of the early eighties, you know the one. A single DCO with saw, pulse, sub and noise mixed, a high-pass ahead of a four-pole ladder, one envelope, and a two-line chorus at three speeds.

    Pad Strings Lead Saw Lead Bass 106 Bass Keys Electric Key
    52 presets

  • Hexa

    LarkOS's own six-voice wavetable polysynth. Each of its two oscillators reads a wavetable of its own from eighteen that ship, and two LFOs move through them either together or apart, you decide! Over all six voices sits "Hex," a macro control that combines stereo drift of the voices, an ensemble and a soft phaser. The first stretch of Hex's travel lets the voices wander slightly apart in pitch as the phaser fades in, the middle brings up the ensemble which thickens the chord and spreads it wide. Near the top, the tone darkens a little and each note leans in from slightly flat to give it a juicy, molasses-y feel.

    Pad Two Rooms Lead Sync Runner Bass Reso Acid Keys Tine Bloom
    28 presets

  • Ham

    The legendary tonewheel organ accurately modeled with 91 wheels always turning, nine drawbars by footage, single-trigger percussion, scanner vibrato, key click and a preamp. A rotary speaker goes between Brake, Chorale and Tremolo, and each speed can follow the project tempo.

    Jazz Jazz Trio Gospel Gospel Shout Rock Rock Grind Reggae Bubble
    23 presets

  • Rack

    A four-tone sample player in the spirit of Roland's JV-880 rack module, with filter and amp envelopes on every tone and two LFOs. Its sounds come from your own JV-880's ROMs. Note for the legal eagles: LarkOS doesn't supply or direct to ROMs, nor does it contain any Roland code.

    200 patches

  • Plaits with DX7 patch compatibility

    Mutable Instruments' Plaits. We know it, we love it: 24 synthesis engines, eight voices. Its six-operator FM engines read DX7 cartridge banks off the card, four banks of 32, and its wavetable engine takes up to 15 of your own single-cycle waves. So bring your own DX7 patches too!

    Virt Analog FM 2-op Wavetable Chord Modal Drums Bass Drum, Snare Drum, Hi-Hat
    24 engines

  • Rings

    Mutable Instruments' gorgeous Rings resonator, with six models: Modal, Sympathetic, String, FM Voice, Quantized and String+Verb. Four voices with a stereo out path. And note OFF in your track lets the sound ring (ahem), because as the resonators decay they are not cut.

    Modal Sympathetic String FM Voice Quantized String+Verb
    6 models

Also in LarkOS, as in CE: Polyend's own synth plugins (ACD, DIRT, FAT, PERC, CHIP, VAP and WTFM), totally unmodified.

Smaller things

  • Undo goes back 39 steps in a pattern, where stock keeps 19.
  • Jump through long folders. In any file browser, Shift with the arrows or the dial jumps to the next run of names starting with a different character.
  • One loudness for every preset of those four, checked by a test, so changing preset doesn't change the mix.
  • Effects saved with the project. All twenty-three Beads and Cassette values travel with it.
  • Beads as its own stem in stem export, beside the delay, the reverb and the master.
  • The input stays awake when Beads is the only send doing anything.

Rack needs your JV-880

Rack is a player. It contains no Roland code and no samples. The .wavs and patches come from the ROMs of a Roland JV-880 you own, converted into ordinary WAV and patch files for the card.

You need three files from the instrument: jv880_rom2.bin (256 KB), jv880_waverom1.bin and jv880_waverom2.bin (2 MB each), the same dumps JV-880 emulators use. LarkOS doesn't supply them, and this page won't point you to anywhere that does. But here's a nifty tool to convert your ROMs!

Rack card builderRuns entirely in this page

    What you get

    One zip, about 12 MB, laid out like the card: 129 waves in System/Synths/Samples/RACK and the patches in System/Synths/Patches/RACK, banks I, A and B converted from the instrument's 192, each levelled to sit at one loudness, plus bank L. Unzip it and copy its System folder onto the card, merging with what is there. The builder is a port of the rack_convert.py and rack_larkos.py scripts, and writes the same files byte for byte.

    Playing it

    On the Tracker, choose Rack as an instrument's model and open Load. Highlighting a patch loads it. Shift with +/- keys jumps to the next bank.

    Expansion boards

    If you also own SR-JV80 boards, rack_expand.py converts them on a computer: up to 19 boards and 3,905 patches, each board a folder in the patch browser. Patches whose waves won't fit the 4 MB sample pool go to a separate Oversize folder, so nothing in the browser fails to load.

    Putting it on your Mini

    Downloads are not open yet

    The first build is being tested on a real Tracker Mini Anniverary Edition before anyone else flashes it on their Mini (or Mini AE). When it's ready, the firmware and the card files will appear on GitHub Releases, always free. I don't intend on developing for Tracker or Tracker+, so this may or may not work for those devices - no guarantees, no support, flash it at your own risk, etc.

    1. Back up the card's Projects folder. CE saves patterns in a newer format that firmware older than CE can't read.

    2. Put a stock .ptf in /Firmware before anything else, so going back is one update away.

    3. Copy TrackerMini_LarkOS_1.0.0.ptf into /Firmware.

    4. Copy the card zip's System folder onto the card, merging with what is there. It holds the plugins and their presets.

    5. Open Config › Firmware › Firmware update, choose the LarkOS file and confirm.

    6. If you see the LarkOS startup screen, you're in!

    7. For Rack, add your converted ROMs with the card builder above.

    If it won't start

    Put a known-good .ptf in /Firmware, ideally the only one there, and insert the card. Press the recessed reset button next to the USB-C port with a paperclip: the bootloader picks up the file and flashes it. The bootloader sits apart from the firmware, so this works however badly a build behaves.

    Going back to stock

    Flash the stock file the same way you flashed LarkOS. Projects open on both, and the effect settings file is simply ignored by firmware that doesn't know it. Chords that use LarkOS's extra chord entries won't play as written on stock.
